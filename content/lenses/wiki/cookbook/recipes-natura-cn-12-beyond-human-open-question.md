Not a recipe, and the first line says so. The header every other chapter carries is deliberately dropped, because this one builds nothing and must never be read as though it did. It contains zero UNI claims, and it restates the programme's own position unsoftened: a developmental active-inference simulation, a toy world, never a person. No claim is made about whether any successor to the human is possible, impossible, near or far. The one job it does is to map the constraints a candidate would have to satisfy, and to state what would refute each claim people make about the subject.

The question, as usually asked, is ill-posed — and that is treated as good science rather than evasion. The phrase presumes a single ladder with humans on a rung. Evolution has no such ladder; it has a landscape defined relative to an environment, and the landscape moves. If you cannot name the environment, you cannot name the fitness, and the word beyond has nothing to refer to. Such a claim is not weak; it has no truth conditions.

What is real is a constraint space, and that part is measurable. Heat, the geometry of wiring against delay, and development are the limits that actually bind — not a shortage of ambition.

This chapter is a register entry for a permanent open question, and its whole design is about not becoming a plan. It carries no building brief, no roadmap, and no target, and states that there must never be one. The science is cited from other people's work, so it is not one of the project's own results. It repeats the programme's own position, and does not soften it: a simulation, a toy world, never a person. And it makes no claim about whether any successor to the human is possible.

Its most useful section is the correction it opens with. Asking what lies beyond a species presumes a scalar ladder, and evolution provides none, because fitness is defined relative to an environment that itself keeps moving. An organism that lost an organ is not less evolved than its ancestor; it is differently adapted, and has had exactly as long to get there. The chapter then gives the sharpest available statistical correction: complexity has a floor below which nothing viable can go, so a random walk that starts against that floor will extend its upper tail over time without being directional at all. The commonest state of life on this planet has not moved. Reading progress off the growth of the tail is reading a boundary condition as a driving force.

The rest is constraints, each sourced and each with a falsifier. Heat is one, and the chapter is careful to say it is heat rather than the theoretical floor on the cost of erasing information, because that floor is so distant it constrains nothing. Signal speed against size is another, and the chapter presents its wiring model as the weakest thing it carries, explaining that the model predicts modularity rather than impossibility, and naming what would refute it. Information, development and metabolism follow. The metabolic section carries an honest disconfirmation of a famous and elegant hypothesis, which did not survive a broad comparative test. The chapter records it as first-class precisely because it is a hypothesis the wing would have liked to be true.

There are sections on which gradients are actually real, on what would have to be true for any claim here to hold, and on how open questions are carried whole rather than resolved.

The central claim is that no environment-independent scalar exists along which the question could even be posed. The falsifier is written as three conditions any candidate quantity would have to meet. It must be computable from an organism alone. It must order known organisms in a way that survives the counterexamples in the chapter's own table. And not merely a redescription of similarity to humans. The chapter says the third condition is the load-bearing one and that every candidate proposed so far fails it, and that this is its actual finding.

Its negatives are pointed. Total neuron count is shown to have failed twice as a proxy, with two studies in the same journal reaching opposite conclusions. A claim about uploading is marked inadmissible in form rather than rebutted, with the chapter noting that serious people hold the position and that the fence is a request for a falsifier rather than a dismissal. A widely quoted figure for how much a person takes in at once drifts across retellings of a single estimate, and is admissible only as an attributed estimate. And a comparison expressed in orders of magnitude is shown to depend entirely on an accounting choice that is usually left undeclared, which the chapter calls a choice wearing a measurement's clothes.
