Water gets a spec sheet here, as the medium every other nature chapter quietly assumes. Everything in it is cited from chemistry, so it contributes no evidence to the programme's own results. It closes with a list of what is not claimed, starting with any suggestion that water is fine-tuned, purposive, or evidence of anything beyond chemistry.

The argument is that water is no neutral background but a pile of anomalies, and that life is built on them rather than in spite of them. A corollary runs through every section: the right account of what water does is usually about the water, not about the thing dissolved in it. Several familiar popular explanations are marked wrong even where their conclusions are right.

Then the contrast the chapter states outright — water attracts more unearned mysticism than any other substance while being, measurably, stranger than the mysticism. Disputed claims get handled carefully rather than dismissed. One famous result died when the samples were blinded. One controlled study turns out to have measured how appealing chosen photographs looked, rather than any property of water. One much-hyped phenomenon is split three ways: a real effect, a contested explanation, and a health claim with nothing behind it. Collapsing those three into one verdict, in either direction, is itself the defect.
<!--CLEAR-->
This chapter treats water as a component with a specification rather than as scenery. Nobody here measured any of it: the numbers are published chemistry, cited, and not one of the programme's own results. It also ends with a plain list of what it does not claim, beginning with any hint that water is fine-tuned or made for us. Its structural claim is that water's biologically load-bearing behaviour follows from the shape of its hydrogen-bonded network, and that in each case the right thermodynamic account is about the water rather than about whatever is dissolved in it. It is fenced as modelled: individual rows carry their own classes, but the chapter as a whole composes measured constants through stated assumptions.

The sections work through the consequences one at a time. Liquid water is densest a little above its freezing point rather than at it, which is why ice floats and why a lake does not freeze solid from the bottom. It holds a great deal of heat for its volume, which makes it thermal ballast, and the chapter attaches corrections to that rather than leaving it as a slogan. It costs a great deal to evaporate, which is what makes evaporative cooling work. It has a strong surface skin. It dissolves things — and the chapter is careful to say that the popular explanation of dissolution as water simply pulling harder is not the right account. Its network rearranges astonishingly quickly, on a timescale so short that any claim about water holding a configuration has to reckon with it. And the effect that drives assembly of membranes and folding is given at length as, in the chapter's words, the most botched explanation in popular science.

The falsifier is stated structurally rather than row by row. Exhibit a liquid with the same network that lacks the package of anomalies, or one without the network that has them all. Or overturn the thermodynamic account of the assembly effect at small scales. Individual numbers falling outside their stated scope move only their own row.

The recorded negatives are the most instructive part, and the chapter says the receipt is the lesson rather than the verdict. A celebrated claim about water retaining a memory of what had been diluted out of it was re-run in the same laboratory with the same people. The single change that mattered was blinding: with the sample codes sealed, the effect vanished. The chapter draws the careful conclusion — the effect was located in the observer, and the people who found this out did so by running the experiment rather than by arguing. A second claim, about intention changing water, is handled more subtly, because a controlled study exists and reported a positive result. The chapter shows that what it measured was a subjective aesthetic rating of selected photographs, with no physical property of water measured at all, and with the claimant among the authors. A third is split three ways: an interfacial phenomenon that is real and independently replicated, an explanation of it that is contested, and a health claim that has no measurement behind it at all.

The chapter also corrects a widely repeated superlative about water's heat capacity by naming a liquid that beats it, and supplying the condition the usual compilations omit.
