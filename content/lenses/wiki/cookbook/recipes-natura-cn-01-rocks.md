Rocks open the nature sequence, and the first thing to fix is where the chapter sits. Everything in it is cited from geology rather than measured by this programme, and a nature citation is never a UNI gate. It also prints an explicit list of what is not claimed, beginning with any suggestion that a rock senses, remembers or infers anything at all.

That fence is the subject as well as the boundary. A crystal records the conditions it grew in, at extraordinary fidelity, across an enormous span of time — and never reads back what it recorded. Storage is not inference. The posterior lives in the person doing the measuring.

A second trap hides in shared vocabulary. A crystal settling really does minimise a free energy, but not the kind that appears elsewhere in this book. Two quantities wear one name, and sliding from one to the other is a pun rather than an argument.

Every number carries a source you can check or is written down as not measured. Several are recorded as withdrawn or superseded, including one that the chapter's own brief had asserted and instructed be checked.

The nature chapters begin here, with the one that sets the calibration for all the rest. Its subject is the mineral substrate, and its role is to be the reference case for something with a boundary but no model of the world beyond it. The science is borrowed: geologists measured it, this chapter cites them, and it is not one of the programme's own results. Its closing pages carry a long list of what is not claimed.

It begins by showing that even the definition of a mineral frays under inspection. The governing definition is a committee's operational one, hedged on purpose, non-retroactive, and curated — so a species count is the state of a ledger rather than a fact about the Earth.

Then comes the chapter's spine, which is genuinely elegant. One building block, one rule about how those blocks may join, and the entire silicate kingdom falls out with no free parameters. Count how many corners a unit shares and you generate the classes in order, from isolated points through pairs, rings, chains, sheets and frameworks. The chapter then observes that the temperature order in which these minerals crystallise out of a cooling melt — established separately, by melting experiments rather than by structure — is that same order. Cooling is linking. It fences the correspondence carefully: the ordering is observed, the causal story is modelled, and the series applies to one starting composition rather than being a law.

A second theme is scope. The number of essentially different ways matter can be periodically ordered in three dimensions is a closed formal result, which the chapter calls a gift, because it means the space of possible structures is finite and written down. It then spends more time on the counterweight: a phase was found with an order forbidden in every one of those groups, because it is not periodic. The word periodic was an axiom nobody restated, so a replicated observation was dismissed for years for contradicting a result whose premise had gone quiet. That episode is recorded as the reference case for forgetting a scope condition.

Other sections show the same discipline applied to particular numbers. A hardness scale that is ordinal cannot be averaged or differenced, and the chapter supplies a clean rank inversion as the receipt. Unusually, it also names a second inversion it refuses to use, because the sample was the wrong kind, so nobody re-imports it. Two determinations of the same point are carried side by side with an instruction not to average them. The Earth's interior is presented as a posterior conditioned on data and on a parameterisation, with one value flagged as an input the model was handed rather than a result it earned.

The blanket analysis is the chapter's core. A rock has a boundary and internal states, but nothing inside it stands in for anything outside it for its own use; it has no actions, no preferences, and no temporal depth. It is a write-once medium with no reader.

The recorded negatives are unusually candid. A widely repeated claim about how many minerals require life was withdrawn by its own authors, and is recorded as wrong rather than repeated. The corrected version puts water ahead of life, and even that correction rests on one unreplicated survey, with both figures given as lower bounds. Unfalsifiable claims about crystals are recorded without mockery, alongside the real and stranger things nearby. And it closes by saying again that citing geology is not evidence for anything the programme claims.
