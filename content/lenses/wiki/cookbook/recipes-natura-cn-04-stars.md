A provenance chain for the matter a cell is made of is what gets built here — an audit trail running from a proton to a carbon atom to a gold atom, with a source on every link and an explicit mark wherever the chain is open. It raises no rung in the programme's own ledger: astrophysics is cited, not measured here, and the closing fence spells out what the chapter does not claim, including the fine-tuning gloss its most famous story attracts.

Its spine is why heavy elements exist at all in time to matter. Heavier stars burn out far faster than light ones, and the steepness of that relationship is what puts their products back into circulation quickly enough for a later planet to be made of them. The chapter states that as its structural claim and gives the model that would refute it.

It is unusually hard on its own best stories. The famous prediction of a nuclear energy level is corrected: the anthropic version of the tale is marked inadmissible as history, and the real version, resting on a measured abundance, is described as stronger. And a widely repeated claim that a particular observation measured where gold comes from is recorded as false, against the chapter's own commissioning brief.
<!--CLEAR-->
This chapter treats a star as a machine with a specification and traces where the atoms in a cell came from. Its declared structural claim is causal: because a star's lifetime falls steeply with its mass, massive stars finish quickly, and that speed is what makes heavy elements available on a timescale short compared with the age of the galaxy. The falsifier is written out as a specific alternative — produce a self-consistent model of galactic chemical enrichment in which massive stars live as long as the Sun and the observed composition still comes out right, and the spine breaks.

It starts with the one equation that holds a star up: its own pressure gradient against its own weight, with nothing exotic required. Before that, the condition under which a cloud collapses at all, and the chapter openly names the known sleight of hand in the standard derivation rather than hiding it, keeping it as a working criterion while marking it as not rigorous. Then the organising diagram that sorts stars by brightness and colour, and the scaling of brightness with mass — where the chapter corrects the textbook, showing that the single exponent everyone quotes is not any of the calibrated pieces measured across the mass range, and that the sequence does not even move in one direction. It is usable as a rough device and inadmissible as a measurement.

A section is given to the temptation to link this scaling to the way metabolic rate scales with body mass in animals. The chapter refuses it, and explains the refusal: the claim as stated is unfalsifiable, the two relationships bend in opposite directions relative to proportionality, and power laws are the generic output of scale-free constraints anyway. It is recorded as a rhyme, and the refusal is presented as the method.

Then the burning itself: which reactions run at which temperatures, the prediction of a nuclear energy level that made carbon production possible, why fusion stops around iron, and how the elements beyond iron are made by slow and rapid capture of neutrons. The chapter is careful about what a celebrated merger observation showed — a lighter element was identified, while the heaviest ones were searched for and not found, so their origin in such events remains an inference from an abundance pattern rather than a detection. There is a section on the brightness limit above which a star blows its own material away, and one on the way the Sun rings, which is how its interior is inferred at all.

The negatives are pointed. A famous story about anthropic reasoning is corrected as history. A claim about which nucleus is most tightly bound is shown to be true of one quantity and false of another, with the defect being the failure to say which. Several standard values are printed with a flag saying their primary sources were not read in this pass, and three genuinely open problems are printed as contested rather than adjudicated, because the chapter says it cannot adjudicate them.

The fence is modelled: individual rows carry their own classes, but the chapter as an artefact composes measured constants through stated assumptions into a provenance model.
