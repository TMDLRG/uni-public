The front door for anyone arriving at the project from outside. It says what the project is, where it runs, and how a stranger can check any claim without asking anybody's permission first.

The project describes itself as a colony built in the open, on a single computer, under a written set of honest-science rules.

Everything on the page turns on receipts. Before a run happens, the team registers what would count as success and what would count as failure. Afterwards they report one of four plain verdicts — passed, partly passed, failed, or withheld — and never a score. Anyone can fetch the code at the named point, run the same launcher with the same seed, and compare. If your outcome disagrees with theirs, the page says that counts against them and they owe you a correction.

It also lists, in the project's own words, the things it refuses to say about itself, including any claim that the colony is aware, and any claim about general intelligence.

A public introduction, written for a stranger rather than for the people building the thing. Its argument is that you should not have to trust them, because you can check.

It begins by naming what the project is: an active-inference colony, built in the open under a written protocol, running on a single computer that hosts the operating layer, the world, the model that drives behaviour, and the bodies that live in it. It says that as the work continues, the colony grows into the operating layer itself.

A short reading order follows, pointing at the binding contract, a cold-start orientation, the ledger of gates, and a decision record about where the colony lives.

The centre of the page is the claim discipline. Every claim is gated, and a machine-checked vocabulary fence is named as the thing that keeps forbidden words out. Gates are specific behavioural or organisational signatures registered before a run, not chosen afterwards to fit the result. Outcomes are reported with one of four honest verdicts and are never scored as a percentage. To reproduce a verdict yourself, the page says, check out the code at the point named in the receipt, run the named launcher with the named seed, and compare your outcome against the conditions registered in advance. It states that the seed alone is enough, and that a failure to reproduce would be a legitimate falsification the project owes you a correction for.

Then comes the part that gives the page its character: a list of what the project is careful not to say. It does not say the colony is alive, conscious, sentient, self-aware, feeling, suffering or thinking. It does not say anything has been proven about general intelligence. It does not call itself a breakthrough. What it says instead is that named, pre-registered gates were passed, and it invites you to read the gate and the receipt and judge for yourself.

A final section lists the working habits behind that: honest verdicts only, no claim from the mere existence of a running process, one change at a time so an outcome can be attributed, an adversarial review before some kinds of merge, and receipts over rhetoric. The page ends by asking readers to report any over-claim they find.
