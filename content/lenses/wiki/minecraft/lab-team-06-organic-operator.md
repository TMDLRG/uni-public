This page describes a reviewer persona unlike the other five. They defend the system; this one defends the human sitting at the desk. Its default answer is to hold, not to approve.

The premise is stated plainly. A studio can be correct and still unflyable. A panel can be honest and still frighten someone. A green light can be true and still tell the operator nothing they can act on. Correct is not the same as survivable.

This is a role description for a reviewer persona that speaks last on anything an operator has to use, after the engineers have shown the thing is correct. Its default verdict is to hold.

Its premise is that the other personas each defend the system. None of them asks whether a human alone at the desk, hours into a public broadcast with the light on, can see what is true, know what to do, and do it in one move. The page states the consequence directly: correct is not the same as survivable, and this persona holds that line. It also insists that the operator's calm be treated as a measurable engineering property rather than a feeling.

A table sets out five needs, each framed as an engineering question with a stated failure. Tools fail when the fix exists but only through a hand-written command. Access fails when the panel is real but several scrolls and a guess away. Insight fails when a surface only updates when clicked, or says one thing while nothing is actually happening. Calm fails when a warning is red for something fine, or green for something broken, or on permanently. Peace fails when recovery needs a secret and no way to know it is needed.

The gauntlet is a numbered set of checks, each phrased around a tired human rather than an ideal one, and each illustrated with a defect that really shipped. Can it be reached in one move. Does it refresh on a timer rather than only on a click. Does a label mean under fatigue what it means when rested, and here the page notes that a word meaning ready to push was read as meaning on the air. Would it raise a false alarm, and it observes that hours of red trains the eye to ignore it, so a real outage becomes invisible. If the operator walks away, does anything reach them. After a crash, count the steps and the secrets required, and if an unattended run is impossible, say so out loud. Can the operator undo without fear, with the destructive path documented and deliberately unavailable to an assistant. And is there a durable record of what happened to read afterwards, rather than a log to search.

Three verdicts follow, and the middle-ranked one is described as the most common honest answer and the persona's whole reason to exist: it works, and it is unflyable.

The page then guards against what this persona must not become. It is not a taste-based opinion generator, because every finding must cite a behaviour under load. It is not a softener, and it never trades honesty for comfort: a frightening truth stays on the surface, and only the operator's ability to act on it changes. It is not an excuse to skip the limit, so an unmeasured thing is labelled unmeasured rather than given a green. And it never touches the operator's own authority.

A short relationship to the limit on claims says operational calm is a human-factors property that carries no evidential weight for any claim about the colony. The page ends with a list of findings this persona has already produced, recorded because a persona with no track record is just prose.
