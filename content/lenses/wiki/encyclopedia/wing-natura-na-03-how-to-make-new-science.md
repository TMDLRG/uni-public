Method, not evidence — the chapter contributes no evidence to the program's own results and raises no rung of its build. What it offers instead is a checklist a builder could run tomorrow morning. It starts from the asymmetry everything else rests on: any number of agreeing observations leaves a universal claim standing but unearned, while one clean disagreeing observation kills it. So before you run anything, write down the observation that would make you abandon the idea. If you cannot write that sentence, no amount of data will convert you. From there it walks through strong inference, keeping several rival explanations alive at once, severity, pre-registration, touching a held-out set once, tuned baselines, controls and blinding, and the negative result as a result. It closes on how something genuinely new gets made: find a residue the current model cannot absorb, kill the mundane explanations first, and refuse to make it go away. It also names what it does not claim.

<!--CLEAR-->

The procedure at the core of the chapter is strong inference: devise alternative hypotheses, devise a crucial experiment whose alternative outcomes each exclude one or more of them, carry it out cleanly, then recycle. The point of the second step is that an experiment whose every outcome leaves all hypotheses alive is not an experiment but an expense. The advice is to write the tree down as a file before the build, with the outcome-to-exclusion mapping stated in advance, because if that mapping was not written before the run, what happened was storytelling with a dataset.

Plurality is treated as load-bearing rather than optional, with the classic warning that a researcher grows attached to an explanation the moment they offer one, and the remedy of keeping several structurally distinct hypotheses alive at once. Severity is made operable as a single line on a pre-run checklist: if the claim were false, what is the probability this exact procedure would still have passed it?

Three further rules are really one. Pre-register the bar and the stopping rule, touch the held-out set exactly once behind a seal, and read the verdict off the interval bound rather than the point estimate. A famous eclipse measurement shows why, since it severely excluded one prediction while being consistent with, but not pinning, the other. A disclosed exclusion of some plates travels with that result permanently, because an exclusion is not a sin but an undisclosed exclusion is.

Later sections cover the tuned baseline and the discriminator that must collapse a gain, statistics that should scare a reader into method, and measurement and provenance, including an anomaly traced to a loose connector, published honestly and then refuted by its own collaboration. Controls, blinding and the positive control that most people skip get their own section.

The closing section describes how new science is actually made: subtract the best current model, characterise the residual, exhaust the boring explanations, propose competing mechanisms, design the experiment that kills at least one, and publish either way.
