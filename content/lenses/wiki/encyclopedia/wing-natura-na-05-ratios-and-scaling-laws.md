Somebody else fitted every curve in this chapter; it is quoted here, and contributes no evidence to the programme's own results. What it offers instead is a way of thinking about how a quantity changes when size changes. Its one-line rule is that the exponent is the science and the prefactor is the engineering. Change your units and the prefactor moves; the exponent does not. Only the exponent is a candidate statement about nature. The central case is metabolic scaling — real, useful, and genuinely disputed. That dispute is carried at full strength rather than resolved by preference. Careful people fitting overlapping data reach intervals that disagree. One proposed resolution says the relation is a curve, not a power law at all. That resolution is itself under a published objection, with a published reply. All three positions are printed, including the one that suits the chapter's own argument least. A long closing fence then lists, item by item, what is not claimed — beginning with the exponent a reader would most expect it to endorse.

<!--CLEAR-->

The method is stated first: on logarithms an allometric relation is a straight line, and the exponent is dimensionless and unit-invariant while the prefactor is an artifact of your bookkeeping. A sharp corollary follows, that the implicit mass scale is harmless for a line and stops being harmless the moment the model is not a line.

The central dispute is then laid out honestly, with a warning against the error the chapter's own table invites. Rows that look like six corroborations are really a handful of papers over overlapping compilations, and rounding-level agreement is not independent replication. One paper's own unbinned fit rejects the exponent its title argues for, and the headline arrives only after a defensible but discretionary binning choice. Compilations of many relations show a mean near the famous value with a spread running from well below to well above it.

The proposed resolution fits a quadratic in log space and finds convex curvature, so there is no exponent at all but a curve. Fit a line to a mouse-heavy dataset or to a large-animal dataset and you get different slopes, both locally correct and globally meaningless. The chapter computes local slopes at named masses rather than quoting a coefficient it has shown to be scale-dependent, and flags its own last row as an extrapolation beyond the fitted range. Then it carries the objection, and the reply to the objection, at full strength, including the receipts the reply never answers.

Later sections cover where a famous derivation comes from and what its assumptions cost. They cover how little of the quarter-power family survives measurement, since a widely quoted lifetime invariant comes apart when the measured exponents are composed. Then a surface-to-volume rule with a large exception class, and an elastic-similarity model that mostly failed its own test. Last, the diffusion time that forces circulatory systems to exist, worked through with its own condition mismatch declared inside the showpiece calculation.

The operable payload is a short procedure for using a ratio to design. State the target scale and the fitted range. Carry the exponent and read its interval rather than its point estimate. Compute the magnitude, showing the arithmetic. State the falsifier before looking, and check whether the regime changed.

It ends on a fence rather than a conclusion. Item by item it records what is not claimed. Not the famous exponent, and not its rival. Not that the curvature paper closed the argument, and not that a much-repeated lifetime constant is constant. Not that cities and organisms are one kind of thing, and not that a rule of thumb about body size and latitude follows from geometry. The last item governs the whole wing. The chapter contributes no evidence to the programme's own results, because the vocabulary used for nature's regularities and the vocabulary used for its build status describe different kinds of claim and never merge.
